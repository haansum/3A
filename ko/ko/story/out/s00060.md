Laleh Hoang drove away from Coach Dunleavy, and you, Nadezhda Halloran, now train under the coach she walked out on. At the Fort Wayne Open in 2017 she took the van keys from Dunleavy's bag and drove home alone, and you were one of the four who bought a sixty-one-dollar Greyhound seat. Tonight's belt is the one Dunleavy, before he died, said you would win.

*

Round 2, 1:47, and you had been winning it with the jab. Her left shin comes in from your right side, level, hip rolled over, and lands flush on the hinge of your jaw with a sound like a dropped plank. You go over to your right, and you turn as you go, a quarter turn and then more, the toes of your left foot curling under, your shoulders rounding forward and your chin dropping into your chest instead of flinging open, your left leg folding beneath you until the heel tucks against your hip, your head set down on the canvas without a knock, and the turn carries you over onto your left side across the painted free-throw line showing through the mat. Your eyes roll up and off to the left, toward the rafters, a rim of white showing under each.

*

The referee slides in on his knees, a beat late. Laleh steps away two paces, exactly the two she was taught, and no farther. She counts with him, low, under his voice.

"Six. Seven. Somebody tell the Mole she got paid by the round and only worked one and a half."

Your second kneels at your feet and starts on your boot laces first, as if that were the job. The liniment on her hands is the strongest smell in the armory.

Your eyes come down off the rafters and start to follow things. Laleh has turned to the folding chairs at the far end, the twelve-dollar seats, where your students sit in your gym's shirts.

"Your coach told you about the van. Four seats, sixty-one apiece, two hundred forty-four dollars. That's the most she ever charged anybody, and she charged me."

Your students have gone quiet. The other end of the armory keeps cheering for a fight that is over.

"Count's not yours," your second said, not looking up from the laces.

Laleh talks over the referee now. "Nine. Ten."

The timekeeper rings the bell. Nobody hears it. He rings it again, then a third time, hard, and the cheering end finally stops to look.

Laleh wipes her shin on the towel her corner holds out. It smells of liniment too.

"Dunleavy said this belt was worth more than she was. Turns out he had the number right."

She ducks through the ropes while the announcer is unfolding the card, and she is halfway up the aisle, past your students, before he reads her name.
