Forty-one candles would be wrong too, but the cake says 1987 and Coach started the gym in '89, so everybody is being kind about it. I am good at kind. I cut Fenna Quispe a corner piece, the one with the most icing, and carry it over on a napkin.

She doesn't take it. She tells me what Saturday pays: her side, my side, the gap. She has it to the dollar. She says the gap is what Kestrel is worth on a poster and that she wore it first, at sixteen, on a singlet her aunt lettered. She says a debut at home sells six hundred seats and I should thank her for my bonus. She says thank like it has a price.

Around us people are singing to Coach. I lean in the way you do to be heard over singing, smile for the room, and tell her, low, that I counted. Forty people in that speech. I left her out on purpose, and I would again. Then, because it is true and I am polite: you have one good minute, Fenna. After that you are a poster.

She takes the cake then. Eats it looking at me. Okay, Oksana, she says, with her mouth full.

Saturday. Fifty-two seconds on the clock above her shoulder, so I was right, she is breathing through her mouth already, and my hands are where they like to be, by my hips. The heel comes around from my right. I never see it; I hear it, and then I am standing very tall on nothing, tipping, with time to think: forty-one.
